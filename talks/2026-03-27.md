As functional programmers, we like seeing beautiful declarative versions of horribly-looking imperative algorithms. Why? Because separating concerns makes code easier to understand, compose, and trust.

Tarjan's Strongly Connected Components (SCC) algorithm [1] is a prime example of an imperative mess. It uses a tangled state of a mutable stack and two mutable maps of indices, with recursion on top. The algorithm has long resisted a clean functional translation. King and Launchbury in their classic paper [2] describe an elegant, declarative Depth First Search (DFS) framework, and show an SCC algorithm that is composed out of several simpler functions. Alas, their SCC algorithm is strict (it requires the entire graph to compute the answer).
Tarjan's algorithm, which produces SCCs lazily, was not included in their paper (and they did not know how to tackle it declaratively [personal communication]).

In this talk, I will present (what I believe to be) the first cleanly declarative description of Tarjan's SCC algorithm. It is short, easy to understand, and has the same compositional structure as the King and Launchbury algorithms (or at least I tried to, you be the judge). It has the same time complexity as Tarjan's  original algorithm, and also preserves its lazy, on-the-fly properties. I will also show how to use QuickCheck to test the correctness of the algorithm, and show a practical application (confluence testing for rewrite systems).
[1] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarjan%27s_strongly_connected_components_algorithm
[2]  https://launchbury.blog/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/structuring-dfs-algorithms.pdf
